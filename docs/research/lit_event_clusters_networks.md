# Literature gap check: event taxonomies, event waves, and text-based firm networks

Plain-language summary for AlphaBERT. All citations below were checked against search results (title, authors, venue, year); none needed to be marked UNVERIFIED.

## 1. Unsupervised taxonomies of corporate text

- **Bybee, Kelly, Manela & Xiu, "Business News and Business Cycles," Journal of Finance, 2024** (NBER WP 29344, 2021). They fit a topic model to 800,000 Wall Street Journal articles (1984–2017) to turn news into interpretable themes, then show news attention tracks real activity and forecasts market returns (news attention explains ~25% of aggregate return variation) via a text-augmented VAR. This is the template for "let embeddings/topics discover the taxonomy" rather than using a fixed code list — but it's applied to newspaper coverage of the macroeconomy, not firm-level 8-K events.
- **Dyer, Lang & Stice-Lawrence, "The Evolution of 10-K Textual Disclosure: Evidence from Latent Dirichlet Allocation," Journal of Accounting and Economics, 2017 (64(2):221–245)**. LDA with 150 topics over 10-Ks, 1996–2013. Finds most of the well-known trends (10-Ks getting longer, more boilerplate, less readable) are driven by just 3 of 150 topics (fair value, internal controls, risk factors), traceable to specific FASB/SEC rule changes. Key lesson for us: a handful of topics can dominate corpus-level trends, and topic meaning is anchored to a *specific* regulatory era — a taxonomy fit once can silently become stale as rules change.
- **Dolphin, Dursun, Blankenship, Adams & Pike, "Grounded Event Extraction from SEC 8-K Filings with a Fine-Grained Taxonomy," arXiv 2607.08346, 2026 (Massive.com)**. Builds an LLM-based, three-tier taxonomy of 119 event types, tags ~293,000 8-Ks, grounds every tag in verbatim text. Central finding directly relevant to us: **9 of the 15 most market-reactive event types — clinical trial results, merger completions/withdrawals, regulatory decisions, voluntary delistings, patent milestones — are filed mostly under Item 8.01**, the one item code that carries *no* event-type information. This is the sharpest confirmation that SEC item codes are not a usable proxy for "what kind of event happened," and that the market-moving content is concentrated in the catch-all bucket. Directly motivates learning event types from text rather than trusting item codes.
- No paper found that runs unsupervised embedding clustering (as opposed to LDA/keyword taxonomies) specifically on 8-Ks and links clusters to return drift — see gap (a) below.

## 2. Event waves / clustered disclosures across firms

- **Contagion after accounting restatements**: the original finding (Gleason, Jenkins & Johnson-type results, often cited as "The Contagion Effects of Accounting Restatements," The Accounting Review, 2008) is that a restatement announcement drags down non-restating same-industry peers' prices, concentrated in revenue restatements at large firms, and not explained by analyst forecast revisions — consistent with a market-wide "accounting quality" scare. **Lewis-Western, Seidel & Wilkins, "Do Restatements Generate Contagion? A Re-examination," Review of Accounting Studies (also on SSRN, 2024–2026 vintage)** push back on the related claim (from Kedia, Rajgopal & Zhou-type work) that restatements cause peers to *start* misreporting: once measurement-error issues are fixed, they find no evidence of that specific channel, though peers do report less aggressively afterward, especially with high prior accruals or a shared audit office. **Takeaway: the price-contagion effect of bad disclosures spreading to peers is reasonably well established; the deeper claim that it changes peer reporting behavior is contested.**
- **Going-concern opinions**: Elliott, Highfield & Schaub, "Contagion or Competition: Going Concern Audit Opinions for Real Estate Firms," Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics, 2006 (32(4):435–448), and a related 2012 ISEG working paper ("Going Concern Opinions Are Not Bad News: Evidence from Industry Rivals") find *competitive*, not contagious, effects for real-estate rivals — a going-concern opinion for one firm can be *good* news for close competitors (less competition), the opposite sign from restatement contagion. **Lesson: "event wave" signals are not uniformly bad-news-spreads-to-peers; the sign depends on the event type and whether peers substitute for the distressed firm.**
- **Peer earnings-announcement spillovers**: intra-industry information transfer at earnings announcements is a long-standing, well-replicated finding; **Frankel, Godigbe & Rabier, "Information Spillovers at Earnings Announcements," Journal of Accounting Research, 2025 (63(1):319–362)** is a recent, more skeptical entry — they show signed and absolute peer-firm return co-movement is actually *lower*, not higher, right at the focal firm's own earnings-announcement date than on other days, complicating the simple "one firm's news moves its peers" story.
- No paper found that treats an *industry-wide burst in 8-K filing volume itself* (rather than a specific bad-news type like restatements) as a stress/systemic-risk signal — see gap (b) below.

## 3. Text-based firm networks and return spillovers

- **Cohen & Frazzini, "Economic Links and Predictable Returns," Journal of Finance, 2008**. Using disclosed customer-supplier links, a positive/negative shock to a customer predicts the supplier's future return; a long-short "customer momentum" strategy earns >150bp/month alpha, largely surviving standard risk-factor and momentum controls. Establishes that investors under-react to *known, disclosed* economic links.
- **Hoberg & Phillips, "Text-Based Network Industries and Endogenous Product Differentiation," Journal of Political Economy, 2016 (124(5):1423–1465)**. Builds TNIC: firm-pairwise product-market similarity from 10-K product descriptions, giving each firm its own bespoke set of "competitors" instead of a fixed SIC/NAICS bucket. This is the foundational text-similarity-network paper in finance and the direct ancestor of "build a network from filing text."
- **Lee, Sun, Wang & Zhang, "Technological Links and Predictable Returns," Journal of Financial Economics, 2019 (132(3):76–96)**. Builds a technology-similarity network (patent-text based) and shows a focal firm's return lags its "tech-peers'" recent returns — a genuine return-spillover result, ~117bp/month long-short alpha, distinct from industry momentum, stronger when investor attention is low and arbitrage is costly.
- Three very recent (2026) arXiv/IJCAI papers extend this line specifically with LLM embeddings: **Huang, Fan, Hu & Ye, "Cross-Stock Predictability via LLM-Augmented Semantic Networks," IJCAI 2026** builds LLM-embedding-based firm networks and reports improved return predictability versus non-text networks; a companion pair of arXiv working papers ("Supply Chain Propagation of Textual Signals: LLM Embeddings and Cross-Sectional Return Predictability" and "LLM Latent Edge Measurement: Point-in-Time Economic Graphs...") pursue the same idea for supply-chain-style graphs built from disclosure text — these are contemporaneous, not-yet-peer-reviewed working papers, so treat their effect sizes as preliminary, but they show the exact "embedding-similarity network from filings → predict peer returns" idea is now an active research frontier, not untested.

## Bottom line: what's established vs. the gap

**Established:** (i) topic models / embeddings can extract meaningful, forecast-relevant structure from corporate/business text at scale (Bybee et al., Dyer et al.); (ii) SEC item codes, especially 8.01, are a poor proxy for true event type — the most reactive events hide in the catch-all (Dolphin et al., 2026); (iii) bad-news events do spread across industry peers in returns, though the sign and mechanism are event-specific and contested in places (restatements: yes on price, contested on peer behavior; going-concern: sometimes competitive, not contagious); (iv) hand-built or structured text-similarity networks (TNIC, tech-peer networks, supply-chain links) generate real, tradable return-spillover effects, and very recent 2026 work is now doing this with LLM embeddings directly.

**Gap (a) — latent 8-K event types from embeddings, linked to return drift:** No published (peer-reviewed) study clusters 8-K embeddings unsupervised and studies post-filing drift by cluster the way Dyer et al. do for 10-K topics or Bybee et al. do for news topics. The Dolphin et al. (2026) taxonomy paper is the closest analog but is LLM-tagged/supervised-taxonomy, not embedding-clustering, and (per its own framing) doesn't run an asset-pricing drift test. This is genuinely open.

**Gap (b) — event waves as a stress signal:** Contagion is studied per-event-type (restatements, going-concern), never as a *generic* "abnormal burst of 8-K filing activity in a sector" indicator. Nobody appears to treat filing-volume/embedding-density spikes across a peer group as a systemic-stress feature in its own right.

**Gap (c) — 8-K text-similarity spillovers:** Not yet done specifically on 8-Ks (existing text-network work uses 10-K product descriptions or patents, which change slowly). Since 2026 saw the first LLM-embedding-network return-spillover papers (supply-chain and general semantic networks), a monthly 8-K-embedding-similarity network predicting peer returns is plausible and current, but unpublished/unverified as a specific result — it would be a novel contribution, not a replication.

## Practical design advice

1. **Cluster count:** Don't chase a single "true" k. Dyer et al. used 150 LDA topics but found only 3 mattered economically — expect a similarly skewed distribution; pick k via a stability criterion (e.g., topic/cluster cosine-similarity agreement across bootstrap resamples, as in the LDA-stability literature) rather than perplexity alone, and report results for a couple of neighboring k values to show they're not cherry-picked.
2. **Stability over time:** Topic models are known to be unstable to even small document-set changes (removing 50 docs can shift topics materially). Re-fit or re-map clusters on a rolling/expanding window and track cluster identity via nearest-centroid matching across windows; don't assume a cluster labeled "guidance cut" in 2015 means the same thing in 2024 given regulatory drift (cf. Dyer et al.'s finding that new disclosure rules directly reshape which topics exist).
3. **Look-ahead bias — the most important pitfall:** Fitting the taxonomy (LDA/k-means/embedding clusters) on the *full* sample and then backtesting drift within that same sample bakes in look-ahead: the clusters "know" about future disclosure trends. Use a strictly expanding or rolling window — fit on data through month t, apply (assign, don't refit) to month t+1 filings, and only then measure forward drift. This mirrors the general financial-backtesting look-ahead-bias literature and is a known failure mode specifically flagged around LLM/text-based backtests.
4. **Event-wave sign is not uniform:** Build in the possibility that clustering of same-industry 8-Ks is *competitively* good news for non-filers (going-concern precedent) as well as *contagiously* bad (restatement precedent) — don't hard-code a single sign into the stress signal; let the model estimate it conditional on event type.
